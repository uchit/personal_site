Forward deployed

How I work when I’m embedded.

I don’t advise from outside the team. I sit inside it, take a share of the delivery risk, and leave behind artefacts that keep working without me. The engagements that go well are the ones where that is clear on day one — so here is the shape, including the parts that are inconvenient.

What I need on day one.

None of this is unusual. All of it missing means the first week goes on access tickets instead of architecture — and on a fixed deadline that week does not come back.

  • Read access to the code and the cloud console. Not admin, not write — read. I cannot form a view on a system I am only told about, and the gap between the architecture diagram and the repository is usually the whole problem.
  • One decision-maker I can interrupt without booking a meeting. The highest-value item on this list. On a compressed timeline the bottleneck is never analysis; it is waiting three days for a decision that takes four minutes. I will use this sparingly, and I will need it.
  • The last six months of incidents, and the deploy pipeline. Incidents tell you what actually breaks, as opposed to what the risk register says breaks. The pipeline tells you what the organisation genuinely believes about quality, as opposed to what the policy says.
  • A seat in the delivery team’s standup from week one. Not the steering committee — that matters later and less. If I am not in the room where the work gets described, I am reconstructing it second-hand and I will be confidently wrong.
  • An honest answer to “what has already been tried?” Almost every engagement has a predecessor attempt. Knowing why it stalled saves me a month, and saves you watching me repeat it.

The first two weeks.

Not a methodology. This is what actually happens, in the order it happens, and it looks much the same whether the estate is a payments platform or a lending core.

Days 1–3Orient

Read the system, not the documentation.

Repository, pipeline, incident history, and one deploy watched end to end with whoever normally does it. In parallel: who actually decides things, which is rarely the org chart. By day three I want to describe the system back to the team in their own language and have them agree. If I can’t, nothing after this is trustworthy.

Days 4–7Find the constraint

Separate what is blocking from what is merely annoying.

Everyone will tell you what is wrong, and the list is long and mostly true. The useful question is which single thing, left broken, stops the date being met. Usually it is not technology — it is an undocumented dependency, an unowned decision, or a team blocking another team without knowing it. The tell is where work queues, not where people complain.

Week 2First decision

Force the four or five decisions that are expensive to reverse.

Tenancy, decomposition, how services talk, where state lives, what the audit evidence is. Left alone these get made implicitly by whoever writes code first, and then cost a re-platform to change. I write them down as decision records — context, options, decision, consequences — and explicitly defer everything else. Deferring loudly is as much of the job as deciding.

End of week 2Visible change

Something is measurably different, and it isn’t a document.

A pipeline that gates on something it didn’t, a paved path a second team can use, a policy check that fails a build. Small, but running. If two weeks produce only analysis, the engagement has already started going wrong, and I would rather say so then than at the end.

What I won’t do.

The section most people leave out, which is exactly why it is worth reading. Each of these has cost me work, and I would make the same call again.

Not negotiable
  • I won’t produce a target state nobody has to implement. A strategy deck with no owner, no sequencing and no budget makes everyone feel organised for a quarter and changes nothing. If the work isn’t going to be encoded in platform defaults, policy checks or paved paths, I would rather say so than bill for the artefact.
  • I won’t chair a review board. Architecture as a gate produces resentment and two systems: the approved one and the one that ships. I will run an enablement clinic — bring a problem, leave with a template, an ADR, or the exception process and what it costs — but I won’t be the queue teams learn to route around.
  • I won’t take the shortcut that permanently changes your security posture. There is almost always a way to save a day by introducing a credential that bypasses your access controls, or a permissive policy “temporarily”. Nobody ever removes it. I will build the slower thing and tell you what the day cost.
  • I won’t tell you the date is fine when it isn’t. If the deadline cannot be met, the most valuable thing I can do is say so early, in writing, with the specific scope that would have to move to make it real. Late honesty about a date is worth nothing to anybody.
  • I won’t leave without a named owner for everything I built. An artefact with no owner is decay with a start date. If nobody will own it, that is a signal it shouldn’t have been built — and I would rather discover that in week two than at handover.

What I leave behind.

The test of an embedded engagement is what still works six months after the invoicing stops. Everything below is a real artefact, in your repositories, owned by someone with a name.

  • Decision records for the choices that were expensive to reverse — context, options taken and rejected, consequences. The form is public: the seven decision trees on this site are the same shape.
  • A paved path a second team can use without me. One golden route to production that is genuinely the easiest route, not a mandated one. The reference architectures are the shapes I start from.
  • Policy-as-code for the principles that matter. The top three encoded as machine checks that break a build, and the rest subtracted. A principle nobody can violate without a warning is a principle; everything else is rhetoric. Working example: the OPA bundle I publish.
  • An evidence pipeline, if you are regulated. Audit artefacts produced as a by-product of running, rather than assembled in a panic before a review. That is the difference between passing an audit and surviving one.
  • A named owner per artefact, agreed before I leave — plus the honest list of what I did not finish and what it would take.

What I deliberately don’t leave is a dependency on me. If the answer to “what happens when he goes?” is a retainer, the engagement failed on its own terms.

The shape that works.

So you can tell quickly whether this is a fit.

  • Weeks to a quarter, with a real date attached. Open-ended advisory is not what this is, and I am not especially good at it.
  • Embedded with the delivery team, not reporting alongside it. Remote-first from Melbourne, with the timezone overlap agreed up front rather than discovered in week three.
  • There is an existing team. I amplify a team; I am not a substitute for one. If there is nobody to hand the paved path to, the work has no landing.
  • The constraint is real — a regulator, a board commitment, a customer date. The work is sharper when something outside engineering is immovable.

Where this is a bad fit: a discovery phase with no delivery attached; a target-state document for a transformation nobody has funded; a standing review-board seat; or an organisation that wants the plan without the encoding. Those are legitimate needs. They are somebody else’s engagement.
