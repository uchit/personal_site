Reference Architecture · Agentic Systems

Agentic systems that survive an audit.

Most agent architectures published in 2026 describe a loop: model, tools, memory, repeat. That shape is fine until someone asks “why did it do that, and who allowed it?” — and then it has no answer. This is the shape that does: two clocks, one chokepoint, explicit autonomy levels, and evidence produced as a by-product of running rather than reconstructed afterwards.

Distilled from agentic systems I have designed and built — a long-running engagement orchestrator, an agent run-record and replay system, and a research/ingestion pipeline. The patterns are generalised; the scars are real.

The two structural commitments are the top two boxes: state lives in rows that outlive any process, and every execution passes one chokepoint. Everything defensible about the system follows from those.

What this architecture commits to.

1 · Two clocks, because one mechanism cannot serve both

The business process lives for months. An agent run lives for thirty seconds. Trying to serve both with one mechanism is the most common structural mistake in agentic systems, and it shows up as either a workflow engine holding a process open for a quarter, or a state machine that cannot retry an LLM call.

Tier 1 holds rows, not processes. A declarative graph of nodes with dependencies, an append-only event log, and a reconciler that compares desired state to actual state and enqueues work. Nothing is “in flight” in memory; if the system restarts, the reconciler simply reads the rows again. Tier 2 owns retries, timeouts, fan-out and provider fallback for a single step, and nothing else.

The payoff is that coverage becomes computed rather than remembered. The system can always answer what is done, what is ready, what is blocked, and what has gone stale — which is the first question any reviewer asks and the one most agent systems answer with a shrug.

2 · One chokepoint, or the controls are decorative

Every agent execution passes through a single function, in a fixed order: resolve, authorize, precondition, budget, assemble context, render, generate, validate, persist, gate, emit. Tenancy, confidentiality tier, spend ceiling, output schema, groundedness and autonomy level are all enforced there.

The important property is not the list of checks — it is that there is no second path. The moment a second code path can invoke a model, every control becomes advisory and the audit story becomes “we believe it usually happens.” This is the same argument as a paved path in platform engineering, and it fails the same way when bypass is possible.

Practically: one runAgent(). Direct provider SDK calls outside it are a lint failure, not a code-review preference.

3 · Autonomy as a graded, recorded property

“Is it autonomous?” is the wrong granularity. Autonomy is a level attached to each action type and enforced at the gate: from propose-only, through act-then-notify and act-within-envelope, to act-freely for genuinely reversible internal operations. The level in force is written to the run record, so “what was it allowed to do at the time?” is a query rather than an archaeology exercise.

The gate then has exactly two outcomes. Auto-commit for actions that are reversible, internal and within the level. Review queue for anything irreversible, commercial or client-facing. In regulated settings that split is not a preference — it is what lets you point at a named human for a consequential decision, which is what NIST AI RMF GOVERN-1.3 and EU AI Act Article 14 are actually asking for.

A deliberate non-goal worth stating out loud: no autonomy over external side effects — sending, signing, submitting, paying — until the reversibility work is done. That constraint has survived every version of these systems I have built.

4 · The run record is the audit pack and the training signal

Most systems log the output. That is enough to debug a demo and nothing else. The record that actually works carries: the exact assembled context that went to the model, a hash of it, the retrieved knowledge IDs, upstream run IDs so provenance is traceable across the graph, every policy decision with its reason, a groundedness map of claim to citation plus the unsupported claims, the prompt version, model, provider, tokens, cost and latency.

Two things fall out of that, and this is the part worth internalising: the evidence pack a regulator wants and the dataset your improvement loop needs are the same rows. Teams that build them separately build both badly. Teams that treat the trace as the product get conformity evidence for free.

It also makes replay from any step possible — reconstructing precisely what the model saw at step N, following causal links from a tool result back to the call that requested it, and re-issuing that step with an edited context to see what changes. That is the difference between debugging an agent and guessing about one.

5 · Prompts are configuration, so they live in the database

Prompts versioned in a table rather than baked into a deploy: draft, canary, active, retired, with a canary percentage and an output schema stored alongside. Code holds the seed and the fallback. This is what makes rollback a row update rather than a release, lets a regression be attributed to a specific version, and makes “which prompt produced this output” answerable months later when someone disputes a decision.

Every production trace carries its prompt version. Without that tag, evaluation is anecdote.

6 · Confidentiality is a retrieval tier, not a filter bolted on

Where an agent serves multiple clients or business units, general knowledge and confidential knowledge are separate retrieval tiers, and the tier in force is resolved at the chokepoint from the caller's context — never from the prompt. Moving something from confidential to general is an explicit, reviewed promotion, not a side effect of someone's query.

Underneath, row-level security from the first migration. Not added later, and never a permissive policy “temporarily” — because that is precisely the thing nobody removes.

7 · The loop closes in the schema, not in a slide

Two signals matter more than any offline benchmark. First, what a human changed: field-level diffs between what the agent produced and what was accepted. That is the highest-value correction data available and most systems throw it away by storing only the final state. Second, prediction linked to outcome — an explicit link between what the system forecast and what actually happened.

Those two feed calibration and outcome-weighted retrieval: the things that were right before get surfaced more, and systematically optimistic predictions get corrected. Both write back into the chokepoint's context-assembly step, which is what makes the loop real rather than rhetorical.

Where this fails.

It is too much machinery for one use-case. If you are shipping a single assistant with read-only tools, the chokepoint and the two-tier split are overhead you will resent. The architecture starts paying at the point where a second agent appears, or the first write lands, or someone asks for an audit trail.

Review capacity is the real ceiling. Gating consequential actions behind human approval means throughput is bounded by reviewers. If the approval rate sits above roughly 95%, oversight has quietly become rubber-stamping, and the honest fix is to narrow what gets gated rather than pretend the review is doing work.

The learning loop needs volume. Calibration on a few dozen outcomes is noise. Below a few hundred linked prediction/outcome pairs, build the schema and wait rather than shipping a model that is confident about nothing.
