Case studyApplied AI · Assessment design10,561 questions

You can’t write an exam question about something you half understand.

Anyone can write an essay about agentic engineering. Writing 10,561 defensible questions across five weighted domains is a different test — because every question needs a right answer, three wrong answers that a competent practitioner would plausibly choose, and an explanation of why each is wrong. Getting the distractors right is where you find out whether you actually know the subject.

Why this is the claim, not the artefact.

A blog post about agent orchestration can be right in outline and vague everywhere it matters. An assessment cannot. To set a question you must commit to a single defensible answer, and then to three wrong answers that are wrong for interestingly different reasons — the plausible-but-outdated one, the one that works until production, the one that confuses two adjacent concepts. If you cannot construct those, you do not understand the topic well enough to teach it, and the gap becomes obvious immediately rather than after publication.

Multiply that by ten thousand and it stops being a writing exercise. It becomes a systematic audit of where your own knowledge is thin, and where the field’s knowledge is genuinely contested — because there are topics where I could not write a fair question at all, since competent practitioners still disagree about the answer.

The weighting is an argument.

The blueprint distributes weight across five domains. That distribution is not a neutral description of a syllabus — it is a claim about where the work now is:

Agentic architecture & orchestration — 27% Agent loops, stop-reason control, coordinator and sub-agent patterns, context passing, parallel versus sequential execution, multi-agent design. The single largest block, and five years ago it did not exist as a topic.
Tool design & protocol integration — 18% Tool schemas, tool-call and tool-result handling, protocol servers, clients and transports, robust tool design. The integration substrate — and where most production failures actually originate.
Prompt engineering & structured output — 20% Structuring, few-shot, chain-of-thought, prefill, structured output, anti-patterns. Still substantial — but note it is now a minority of the discipline, not the whole of it.
Context management & reliability — 15% Context windows, caching, token budgeting, compaction, retries and idempotency, evals, production reliability. The smallest weight and, in my experience, the largest source of production incidents.

Agentic architecture plus tool design is 45% of the discipline. Prompt engineering — the thing most organisations still mean when they say “AI skills” and still buy training for — is a fifth. If your internal capability plan is mostly prompt courses, it is calibrated to 2023.

The question distribution says something the weighting doesn’t.

The bank is deliberately uneven relative to the exam weights, and the unevenness is the interesting part:

Context management: 15% of the exam, 4,745 questions. The highest question count against the lowest weight. That is not an accident of authoring — it is because the topic has enormous surface area: every combination of cache boundary, budget, compaction strategy and retry semantics is a distinct failure mode with a distinct right answer. Low weight, high blast radius. This is the part practitioners consistently underestimate.

Configuration and workflow: 20% of the exam, 97 questions. The inverse. High weight, tiny surface area, because the answers are largely knowable and stable — you either know how the configuration works or you look it up once. Weight reflects how often it matters; question count reflects how many distinct ways there are to be wrong. Conflating the two is how training programmes end up teaching the wrong thing at length.

What this is evidence of.

Command, not familiarity Ten thousand defensible items with reasoned distractors is not something you can produce from a reading list. It is the difference between having opinions about agentic engineering and being able to examine someone on it.
A calibrated map of the discipline Weighted domains, scenario-shaped items, and a pass mark — a defensible view of what a competent practitioner in 2026 must actually know, rather than a list of topics.
Content engineering at scale A generation and review pipeline that holds quality across ten thousand items — itself an applied-AI system, with the evaluation problem that implies.

Two things I’d say out loud.

1 · Certification is a lagging indicator, and that is what makes the blueprint useful. A syllabus is a consensus artefact: it only contains what enough people agree is settled. So the shape of an exam blueprint tells you which parts of a young discipline have stopped moving — and by subtraction, which parts are still argument. In 2026 the settled part is orchestration and tool design. The unsettled part is evaluation: I could write questions about how to run evals, and almost none about what a good score means.

2 · The teachable surface and the dangerous surface are not the same. The domains that are easy to examine are the ones with crisp right answers. The ones that cause outages — context budgets, compaction, retry semantics under partial failure — are examinable only in fragments, because the real answer is “it depends on your system.” Any capability programme built purely from what is easy to assess will train people for the wrong half of the job.

Why this is here. Not as a product. It is on this site because it is the most direct evidence I have of command over agentic engineering as a discipline rather than as a set of opinions — and because the two arguments above are ones I will defend in a room. If you want the underlying position rather than the artefact, that is what the agentic reference architecture and the agent-controls dataset are for.
